Philip Fong/AFP via Getty Images U.S. stocks steadied Friday after the sharpest two-day slide in artificial intelligence shares in years, though the S&P 500 still finished its worst week since the April 2025 tariff shock. The S&P 500 rose 0.3% to 7,376.86 and lost 2.7% for the week, the Nasdaq composite gained 1.1% to 24,579.42 and the Nasdaq-100 rose 1.35%, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 237 points, or 0.5%, to 51,285.73. Chip and AI stocks led the bounce, with AMD up about 11%, Micron up about 5% and Nvidia up nearly 3%, but the Nasdaq composite is still roughly 10% below its Oct. 6 record after a weekly loss of 3.4%. The relief followed a selloff that began on Wall Street and spread across Asia on Friday, with South Korea's Kospi and Japan's Nikkei 225 each closing down about 6% and Taiwan's Taiex down about 4.5%, as investors weighed whether Democratic control of Congress will slow the construction of data centers. The Kospi fell more than 8% at one point, triggering a 20-minute trading halt, and the Nikkei posted its worst week since 2020. A report showed that employers added 165,000 jobs in October, far more than expected, which lifted bets on a Federal Reserve rate increase in December. U.S. stocks plunged Thursday in their worst session since the April 2025 tariff shock, as investors dumped artificial intelligence and chip stocks on fears that Democrats' sweep in the midterm elections will slow the construction of the data centers that power the AI boom. The S&P 500 fell 4.4% to 7,353.73, the Dow Jones Industrial Average lost 1,833 points, or 3.4%, to 51,523.06, and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 5.5% to 24,315.00, putting it in correction territory, more than 10% below its record close of Oct. 6. All 11 sectors in the S&P 500 closed lower, and roughly $2.5 trillion in market value was erased from the Nasdaq. The selloff reversed Wednesday's rally, when the Dow rose 1,569 points on relief that the election had produced a clear result. Under the surface, however, chip and data center stocks had already been falling, and the selling intensified Thursday. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 12.2%, its worst day in about six and a half years and 32% below the peak it set a month ago. Nvidia fell 9.3%, erasing its gains for the year and removing more than $440 billion in market value. Broadcom dropped 10%, Qualcomm 9.5%, AMD and Micron each about 17% and SanDisk 22%. The seven largest technology companies lost more than $1 trillion in combined value, and the Cboe Volatility Index jumped about 70% to 28.18, its highest close since March 30. Analysts pointed to politics. "The trigger for these moves appears to be the concern that the Democrats, both in Congress and at the state level, will seek to regulate the AI industry and restrict data center development," Joe Maher, a markets economist at Capital Economics, said in a report. Rising electricity prices turned the rapid construction of power-hungry data centers into a campaign issue, according to Jakub Rochlitz, a market analyst at eToro, and some Democrats called for restrictions or pauses on new projects. Texas Gov.-elect Gina Hinojosa, whose state is a hub for planned data center capacity, campaigned on a temporary moratorium on new data centers and an end to certain tax incentives for companies that build them. The data center issue is not limited to one party: Republican Gov. Greg Abbott has paused approvals for new large data center loads connecting to the Texas grid, and conservatives have raised concerns about property rights, electricity prices and local control. "This feels like the capitulation phase for the data center capex beneficiary stocks, especially the semiconductors," said Jed Ellerbroek, a portfolio manager at Argent Capital Management. The facility, which will span roughly 4 million square feet and be operated by Oracle for OpenAI, is expected to be fully completed by the end of 2026. Investors sold AI and chip stocks on Thursday on fears that Democrats’ midterm wins will slow data center construction. Brandon Bell/Getty Images The damage spread beyond chipmakers. Data center operators IREN, Applied Digital and Nebius Group each fell more than 14%. GE Vernova, which supplies power equipment for data centers, dropped more than 7%, and Caterpillar lost 5% on concern that slower data center demand could hurt construction spending. About 35% of the stocks in the S&P 500 and 58% of those in the Nasdaq-100 are now at least 20% below their 52-week highs. Keith Lerner, chief investment officer at Truist Wealth, said the size of the technology selloff was feeding into the broader market. Other assets moved in the opposite direction. The yield on the 10-year Treasury note fell to 4.84% from 4.90% on Wednesday, its lowest level in two months, as investors sought safer holdings, and short-term yields fell about 12 basis points. Oil rose 3% to 4% on renewed concern about supply disruptions from the Iran war. Bitcoin fell about 6% to roughly $75,000. Stocks in Europe fell 0.4% and in Japan 1.8% on Thursday, and European stocks opened lower on Friday. In Seoul, the Kospi dropped below 5,000 for the first time in 10 months during trading, with Samsung Electronics down about 10% and SK hynix about 12% in early trading, before the index closed 6% lower. The Nikkei closed down 6% at 57,434.77, below 60,000 for the first time in nearly half a year. Analysts said the scale of the declines, which followed two days of weakness in AI names, suggests a change in sentiment. On Friday the dollar index rose 0.74%, its biggest one-day gain since June 17, and Treasury yields rose after the jobs report, with the 10-year note near 4.87%. The Nikkei closed down 6% at 57,434.77. Andrew Caballero-Reynolds/AFP via Getty Images Not everyone sees the move as justified. Ed Yardeni, president of Yardeni Research, said a pullback in the AI trade and obstacles to building data centers could offer a silver lining, and he said the economy would benefit from some slowdown in the buildout. Another analyst cautioned against treating the selloff as evidence that demand for AI is disappearing, noting that companies with strong balance sheets and established earnings could prove more resilient. The Democratic agenda for the new Congress has so far focused on health care costs, tariffs and the Iran war, and party leaders have not announced legislation to restrict data centers, though several newly elected governors and state lawmakers have taken positions on them. Traders work on the floor of the New York Stock Exchange on Nov. 6, 2026, a day after a steep selloff in AI and chip stocks. Stocks steadied by the close, with the Nasdaq composite up 1.1% and the S&P 500 up 0.3%. Spencer Platt/Getty Images The shock comes as the labor market and inflation outlook are shaping the Federal Reserve's next move. The Labor Department reported Friday that employers added 165,000 jobs in October, well above the 59,000 economists expected, and that the unemployment rate fell to 4.1% from 4.2%. Average hourly earnings rose 0.4% on the month and 3.1% from a year earlier, above forecasts of 0.3% and 2.9%, and payroll gains for August and September were revised up by a combined 84,000 jobs. Wage growth remains below inflation, which rose to 3.7% in September, and wages have grown more slowly than prices since March. Private employers added 86,000 jobs in October, according to a payroll processor's report, above the 75,000 economists expected. The Fed left its benchmark rate in a range of 3.75% to 4.00% at its meeting last week, after raising it in September, and it said inflation remains elevated. Economists expect another quarter-point increase at the Dec. 9 meeting, and traders put the odds at about 76% to 77% after Friday's report, up from about two-thirds after Thursday's selloff and down from more than 80% a day before that. A survey of service companies showed that the prices they pay for inputs eased to a still-high 73.0 in October from 74.0 in September. Florian Ielpo of Lombard Odier Asset Management said the jobs report underscores the economic resilience the Fed has pointed to and virtually cements the case for a December increase. The stock plunge adds a new variable, since a prolonged drop in equity prices can tighten financial conditions and has historically influenced Fed officials' judgments. The next signals come quickly. Investors will watch whether chip stocks hold Friday's bounce after a drop of this size, how next week's inflation data shapes the odds of a December rate increase, and whether Democratic leaders in Congress or in key states put forward specific data center proposals that give investors something firmer to price. The week leaves the S&P 500 about 5.7% below its record and the Nasdaq composite roughly 10% below its Oct. 6 high, and the question that set off the selloff, how much a Democratic Congress and new governors will constrain the AI buildout, remains unanswered.
