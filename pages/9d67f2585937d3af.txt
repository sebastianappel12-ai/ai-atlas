New data centers would pay roughly $55 billion in transmission charges over 10 years under the Senate-passed Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA), including about $35 billion more than they would owe without it, consulting firm Grid Strategies estimated. The House has yet to vote on it, and federal regulators would write the rule setting the charges only if President Donald Trump signs it. Data centers currently pay around $2 billion a year to use the lines. The bill would mean “an annual increase of approximately $3.5 billion” compared with no bill at all, “yielding an additional $35 billion collected over 10 years,” Grid Strategies wrote. That $35 billion is part of the $55 billion. “The proposed changes in the bill mean data centers will more than pay for their use and upgrades to the transmission system,” Judge Glock, the Manhattan Institute’s director of research and a senior fellow, said in a statement to the Daily Caller News Foundation. The White House supports the data center language. The bill would make Trump’s Ratepayer Protection Pledge “the law of the land,” the White House Office of Management and Budget said in a Nov. 9 statement of administration policy on S. 5653, the Senate bill. “This Administration has consistently insisted that the costs of serving hyperscale load must be borne by those who cause them, not socialized to households and small businesses,” the statement said. “S. 5653 writes that principle into Federal law.” (RELATED: Data Centers Could Get Stuck Paying For Grid Upgrades) “BAAJA would end data center free-loading and require current and future data centers to pay for the upgrades to the grid that their huge electricity consumption requires, without passing off any of their costs to consumers, which they are currently doing,” Democratic Rhode Island Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse said in a Monday statement that cited the Grid Strategies numbers. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) would have to rewrite a pricing policy it set in 1994 so that utilities bill data center customers for the “embedded cost” of the grid that already exists, plus “a reasonable assessment” of the “incremental cost” of new lines built for them, the Senate-passed text states. Charging both is known as “and” pricing, and FERC has not allowed it because the agency found it would bill customers more than the service costs, the Grid Strategies paper said. Republican West Virginia Sen. Shelley Moore Capito’s office did not provide a comment on the record, and FERC did not respond to the DCNF’s request. Whatever data centers pay in incremental costs would then be credited against the rest of a utility’s revenue needs “to prevent double recovery from other customers,” the text states. That credit is how other customers could come out ahead, since the money data centers pay “would be used to reduce transmission costs charged to all other ratepayers,” the paper said. “This does not mean that new data centers could not still raise electricity prices for customers, because the overall increased demand for generation could still drive-up prices,” Glock said. The Grid Strategies paper cites a 270-day deadline for FERC to act. FERC would get just 90 days under the Senate-passed version, which also gives the agency a year after its proposal to issue a final rule. The 270 days came from the September version of the bill, and a managers’ amendment released after the paper cut it to 90, Zach Zimmerman, Grid Strategies’ director of research and policy and a co-author of the paper, said in a statement to the DCNF. “I do not believe this change meaningfully impacts our estimate,” Zimmerman said. “The faster implementation helps ensure the changes are in place for the new data centers coming online.” The estimate rests on roughly 40.3 gigawatts of new data centers hooking up outside Texas during the decade, every one of them buying firm transmission service, Grid Strategies wrote. Texas was left out because FERC has not traditionally regulated transmission service on most of the state’s grid. The $55 billion leaves out data centers that are already connected, Zimmerman told the DCNF. The number is “a rough estimate,” with “significant uncertainties” about how many data centers the new FERC policy would reach, the authors wrote. The paper’s title page credits “support from Arnold Ventures.” A second part of the bill covers retail power bills set by the states. For data centers that connect after the bill becomes law, “No portion of the retail incremental cost may be allocated to, or recovered in the rates of, any customer other than a computational load owner or a computational load operator,” the text states. States could still collect more than that from data centers and “credit those amounts to other customers as a rebate or a reduction” to utility costs, the text states. Deals that states approved before the bill becomes law “shall remain in effect.” States can revise them to fit the new rules where their own laws allow. Charges for power plants under the retail section could total $136 billion over 10 years if every state took them up, a figure the Grid Strategies paper called “an indication of the potential magnitude of the charges rather than a precise estimate.” States “are not required” to adopt those policies, it added. The Electricity Customer Alliance, whose members include tech companies along with household and factory power users, has endorsed the bill, Latitude Media reported in October. “They are willing to pay more than their own costs to make sure other customers are treated fairly,” Jeff Dennis, the alliance’s leader, told the outlet. At the time, the Data Center Coalition, which is the industry’s main trade group, was still looking over the bill and had not endorsed it, according to Latitude. Google, acting outside the bill, has offered to pay for a grid upgrade by itself. It would cover the full $10.8 million in added costs of putting higher-capacity wire on 15.5 miles of old power lines in Columbus, Ohio, Google and Ohio Power Company announced Nov. 19. The work piggybacks on a project the utility had already planned, and the Public Utilities Commission of Ohio and the Ohio Power Siting Board still have to approve it. The data center section “prevents data center developers from offloading those costs onto American consumers,” Jason Hayes, a senior research fellow for energy and environment at the Heritage Foundation, wrote in a Wednesday op-ed for The Daily Signal. “That is a solid principle: Identify who creates a cost and who receives a benefit, then bill them accordingly.” “If ratepayers should be protected from data center costs that provide no direct benefit for them, they should also be protected from the costs of building new transmission lines to connect wind and solar installations they did not approve or vote for,” Hayes said in a statement to the DCNF. Data centers would overpay for power lines under the bill, but that may be the price of getting them built, Travis Fisher, director of energy and environmental policy studies at the Cato Institute, told the DCNF. The arrangement is like a costly trip abroad in which a rich friend buys his own ticket and part of everyone else’s so the whole group can go, Fisher said. Data centers stuck with the whole bill would also have more reason to avoid setting off costly grid work, Fisher said. More of them would likely make their own power on-site or build outside the U.S. if new lines are not built, he added. “At the margin, the higher charges should push some developers toward off-grid or behind-the-meter solutions, particularly where interconnection queues are long,” Alex Stevens, the Institute for Energy Research’s manager of policy and communications, said in a statement to the DCNF. Republican Missouri Sen. Josh Hawley, who voted “no” on BAAJA, said the bill was “stuffed with giveaways to data centers and industry at the expense of farmers,” Utility Dive reported. Sixty-two senators backed the bill on Nov. 19 and 33 opposed it, Senate records show. Capito introduced the bill Sept. 30 with Whitehouse, Republican Utah Sen. Mike Lee and Democratic New Mexico Sen. Martin Heinrich, her committee announced. Environmental groups lobbying against the bill worry that House Speaker Mike Johnson could seek its passage through a fast-track process that requires a two-thirds vote, the Washington Examiner reported Tuesday. By Wednesday, the House had put the Senate-passed bill on its floor schedule for the week of Nov. 30, as an item it may consider under a rule instead of the fast-track process, the chamber’s floor website shows.
