Santa Clara, California, USA - 2021 Share on Facebook Share on Twitter The FINANCIAL — Nvidia faces a reported stockpile of about 600,000 H200 artificial-intelligence chips in Hong Kong awaiting shipment to mainland China, an indication of the obstacles remaining between American export authorization and Chinese market access. Reuters reported the figure on November 5, citing two people familiar with the matter. The report said Beijing had not approved large-volume imports, although some chips had entered mainland China in recent months. Several major Chinese internet companies had each obtained about 10,000 H200 processors, one of Reuters’ sources said. Those quantities are source-based estimates, rather than figures disclosed by Nvidia or the Chinese government. Nvidia, Alibaba, ByteDance, DeepSeek and Tencent did not respond to Reuters’ requests for comment, the news agency said. Neither did China’s Ministry of Industry and Information Technology or its National Development and Reform Commission. The report did not establish when the chips awaiting shipment might be cleared. The latest account follows a constraint Nvidia had already disclosed to investors. Its quarterly filing for the period ended July 26 said U.S. licenses allowed small H200 shipments to specified Chinese customers, but Chinese restrictions prevented it from selling all the products covered by those licenses. It said demand for those products had diminished. Shipments under the American licensing programme accounted for less than 1% of Data Center revenue in the quarter ended July 26. The filing also described an inspection requirement before the licensed H200 products could be delivered to customers. Chips brought into the United States for that process were subject to a 25% tariff. Nvidia said it had been unable to pass that cost to customers and did not expect to do so if licensed sales proceeded. Those disclosures predate the November report. They confirm that restricted access had already affected the business, but they do not independently establish the reported 600,000-chip count. The announcement followed President Donald Trump’s December 8, 2025 decision to permit sales to approved Chinese customers. See also Bellway profit rises as 2027 homebuilding outlook weakens The Bureau of Industry and Security described the approach as a controlled opening. Applicants had to show that exports would not reduce semiconductor production capacity available to American customers. Chinese purchasers also had to adopt export-compliance procedures, including customer screening, and products had to undergo independent testing in the United States. The department presented those conditions as protections for national security and the American technology ecosystem. Its policy announcement concerned whether Washington would authorize exports. It did not promise that Chinese authorities would approve the corresponding imports or that a customer would complete a purchase. BIS linked its January decision to the aim of preserving American leadership in artificial intelligence. The agency said export controls should change as technology develops while continuing to protect national security. That rationale accompanied a conditional licensing process, with individual applications still subject to review. The rule published in the Federal Register on January 15 set out a shift from a presumption of denial to discretionary review for qualifying products. It covered China and Macau, and identified processing performance and memory-bandwidth thresholds rather than granting approval to every product sold by a named company. The rule required applicants to provide technical specifications and data on shipments to commercial customers in the United States. They also had to certify that authorized exports would not delay existing or new U.S. orders or divert foundry capacity otherwise available for comparable or more advanced products for American end users. Another condition limited the aggregate processing performance of qualifying exports to China or Macau to no more than half that of the same computing products shipped to American customers for domestic use. The measure concerned aggregate processing performance, rather than simply counting the number of chips. Small shipments had begun months earlier In July, a senior American official publicly acknowledged that some H200 exports had started. Jeffrey Kessler, the Commerce Department’s under secretary for industry and security, told the House Foreign Affairs Committee that the number shipped to China was very small, Reuters reported on July 14. Kessler said the department had given Congress a confidential list of H200 applications and their status, without discussing that list publicly. The statement established that the programme had produced some deliveries. It did not describe a broad reopening of the Chinese market or provide a number comparable with the November stockpile estimate. See also Blackstone distributable earnings fall as investment exits weaken The July hearing also exposed disagreement in Washington. Representative Gregory Meeks, the leading Democrat on the committee, criticized the administration’s approach to export controls and its approval of advanced-chip licenses for China. Kessler defended enforcement of existing restrictions. Their exchange illustrated the policy dispute surrounding sales that remained subject to government authorization. Kessler also told lawmakers that further regulatory action on chips and AI was coming. He opposed replacing the Biden administration’s earlier AI diffusion rule with a similar framework, saying it would impede the global AI ecosystem. Those comments described his position at the July hearing. A large business with constrained China access Nvidia’s wider financial results show why the China issue cannot be read as a measure of its entire business. In its August 26 earnings announcement, the company reported $96.2 billion of revenue for the quarter ended July 26, up 106% from a year earlier and 18% from the preceding quarter. Data Center revenue was $89 billion, an increase of 117% from a year earlier. The company reported a 75% gross margin and diluted earnings of $2.46 a share under generally accepted accounting principles. Those are historical quarterly results, not an estimate of sales or profits attached to the chips described in Thursday’s report. In the same August announcement, Nvidia projected $108 billion of revenue, plus or minus 2%, for its third fiscal quarter. That forecast assumed no Data Center compute revenue from China. The assumption showed that management’s published forecast did not depend on a reopening of that part of the market; it was guidance issued in August, not a newly announced November forecast. Demand, permission and completed sales The report arrived during a broader reassessment of AI-related shares. The FINANCIAL’s November 5 market coverage described declines in semiconductor and data-center companies as investors weighed spending and policy risks. That market context does not establish how much, if any, of Nvidia’s share-price movement was attributable to the Hong Kong report. As of the November 5 report, the companies and Chinese agencies approached by Reuters had not provided a public response to its questions. The report gave no confirmed timetable for large-scale clearance of the H200 shipments.
