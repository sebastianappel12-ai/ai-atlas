Updated: 5:39 PM EST Nov 5, 2026 Editorial Standards ⓘ Christopher Salas White House Correspondent Charlee Caudill Producer WASHINGTON —Wall Street saw a sharp selloff Thursday, with all three major stock indexes falling as investors questioned whether the artificial intelligence boom that has helped power the U.S. economy is running out of steam. The losses in the stock market come just two days after voters across the country delivered a setback to the data centers needed to sustain that boom. Advertisement "Opposition to data centers is perhaps one of the most surprising stories of 2026," said economic analyst Mark Hamrick. Hamrick said investors had largely assumed there would be few obstacles to the rapid expansion of AI infrastructure. But Tuesday's election results have raised new questions. "The stock market had been basically moving forward on the expectation that there was very little to restrict the data center build-out and the expansion of artificial intelligence," Hamrick said. In three states with significant data center development: Ohio, Georgia and Texas, voters in those states elected governors who campaigned to temporarily halt new construction. The opposition extends well beyond those states. According to Ballotpedia, voters from rural Florida to urban California sided with greater restrictions on data center development in 46 out of 47 ballot measures decided this year. But that pushback could come with an economic cost. "The data center and artificial intelligence build-out has been a key factor in the growth of GDP in our country," Hamrick said. Economists at J.P. Morgan estimate AI-related investment has accounted for nearly a quarter of America's economic growth over the past year. President Donald Trump has warned that communities rejecting data centers risk becoming "backwards and poor." In a statement to Hearst Television, White House spokesman Matthew Middleton said Trump is "committed to maintaining American leadership in SI (Super Intelligence), which will continue to fuel revenue growth, innovation, and productivity across our economy." The White House argues data centers create jobs and generate substantial tax revenue for local governments. It pointed to Virginia, where data centers have generated more than $2.6 billion in state and local tax revenue, and Loudoun County, where they contributed $875 million in taxes in 2024. But critics have raised concerns about environmental impacts, how many permanent jobs data centers actually create, and rising electricity costs. "People are upset with inflation and this has been a contributor to the rise in electricity costs," Hamrick said. The administration said data centers should cover their own electricity costs rather than passing those expenses on to American families. The White House said its voluntary Ratepayer Protection Pledge now includes hundreds of participating organizations, although those commitments do not guarantee electricity bills will remain unchanged. A different place to build Interior Secretary Doug Burgum told Hearst Television that the administration is exploring an alternative: building more data centers on "vast amounts of federal land." "We're looking at every opportunity to help site those because again, it's a question of national security," Burgum said. "We must have them, but they certainly don't have to be in people's backyards." Burgum pointed to remote locations such as North Dakota and Alaska, where colder weather could reduce cooling costs and relatively inexpensive electricity could make operations more affordable. Congress could become the next battleground Democratic lawmakers have already introduced proposals ranging from a nationwide moratorium on new data center construction to legislation requiring technology companies to shoulder more of their electricity costs. Those proposals could receive renewed consideration under the incoming Democratic majorities. But even if Congress passes new restrictions, Trump would still have to sign them into law, or lawmakers would need enough votes to override his veto. That sets up a potential confrontation between an administration determined to accelerate America's AI expansion and voters increasingly concerned about what that expansion means for their communities.
