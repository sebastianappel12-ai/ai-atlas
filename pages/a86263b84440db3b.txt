"/> Top Story Lowry: Jensen Huang and the great Chinese heist By Rich Lowry Nov 26, 2026 20 hrs ago Facebook Twitter WhatsApp SMS Email [+] Add Post and Courier News on Google Rich Lowry Facebook Twitter WhatsApp SMS Email Print Copy article link Save There have been surprises in the Nvidia saga — not least the shocking return of Jensen Huang — but what's unsurprising is that China was using underhanded means to acquire U.S. technology. This is a core competency of the Chinese state. The effort began upon the normalization between the United States and China in 1979, as the Chinese immediately set about ripping off McDonnell Douglas technology to improve their military aircraft. The scale of China's theft of U.S. intellectual property over the decades is almost unfathomable, and it's an ongoing, comprehensive campaign of the Chinese government. China's spy agency, the Ministry of State Security, employs as many as 800,000 people by one estimate, an appropriately gargantuan enterprise for a totalitarian state with sweeping international reach and ambitions. Although it's a matter of debate whether Jensen Huang's love interest, Alice Huang, was a formal "spy," she was in touch with an officer of the Ministry of State Security from the spring of 2025 until this fall, according to a New York Times report. Evidence suggests that her business partner and, as The Times puts it, the man "who was often introduced as her husband," Kang Wei, worked with the agency. Alice Huang was associated with a business called Megaspeed that used Nvidia chips in data centers in Southeast Asia, giving Chinese concerns remote access to them. It also may have been part of a smuggling operation to send semiconductors to China. For his part, Kang was creating a $9 billion Chinese data-center network backed by the government. They were thus at the center of a business of the highest interest to the Chinese spy agency. It has made "counterblockade" work one of its top priorities, assisting Chinese companies in acquiring and smuggling advanced chips. Chen Yixin, the head of the ministry, has written of the agency's mission to "deepen the struggle against blockades, and comprehensively advance artificial intelligence innovation, industry development, and applications." In other words, the Ministry of State Security would be falling down on the job if it didn't have its hooks into Alice Huang and her business partner, as it does in so many others. The National Intelligence Law of 2017 imposed an obligation on all Chinese citizens and organizations to cooperate in intelligence work. It's as if the United States adopted a rule mandating that all Americans cooperate with the CIA. The decades-long Chinese heist has been multifaceted. It has involved cybertheft, leveraging Chinese employees in the United States to pass along information, learning know-how from the U.S. employees of Chinese subsidiaries, using Chinese academic institutions and students to penetrate U.S. companies, exploiting joint ventures, physically stealing technology, investing in and buying U.S. companies or manufacturing their products, and, of course, using every possible means to pressure U.S. entities into sharing intellectual property to gain access to the Chinese market. All of this has been enormously successful. David Shedd, the former acting director of the Defense Intelligence Agency, calls it "the greatest transfer of illicit wealth in human history." It is one thing to potentially lose a technological competition to a rival power that is more talented and innovative; it is another to fall behind a competitor that relies on state-supported theft to gain the upper hand, taking advantage of our naivete and openness. What to do? In a 2025 report, the Information Technology & Innovation Foundation recommends pre-emption as the best tool to counter the Chinese. U.S. companies need to better understand their vulnerabilities and bolster their defenses, while we should, among other things, be blacklisting Chinese universities whose students are involved in industrial espionage, and monitoring and restricting Chinese investment and R&D in the United States. We all know the famous quote attributed to Lenin, "The capitalists will sell us the rope with which we will hang them." The Chinese version implicitly adds that whatever rope we don't sell, they will steal through any means necessary. Rich Lowry is on X @RichLowry.
