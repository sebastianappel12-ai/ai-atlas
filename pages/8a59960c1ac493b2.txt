A Democratic Party sweep took both the House and the Senate in a contest that Republicans now concede, with reluctance, as historic. Trump himself acknowledged that the Iran war played a role in the defeat. That, and the hope that Congress would withhold funding for the war, seems to have enthused the markets to bet on a sudden eruption of peace, and Brent crude fell to $94 a barrel. An FT story, free for Moneycontrol Pro subscribers, said, a bit prematurely, that the “Blue wave sweeps markets back towards normality”. (“Normality” is a bold word to use in the same week as a 9% intraday fall in South Korean markets). US bond yields fell, as markets welcomed a government gridlock that would prevent a widening of an already gaping fiscal deficit. The dollar weakened, raising the hopes of other countries that funds would now flow out of the US. Markets celebrated all this, despite my colleague Abhijit Dutta’s timely warning that “market mood is fragile, and it can swing the other way if Trump tries to double down on his extant policies”. And then, with the peace dividend barely booked, came the other half of the week’s mood: the AI apocalypse. A market that had just cheered the end of one catastrophe found another. Chip stocks took the brunt of the selling. Major investors are now said to be increasingly sceptical that Anthropic’s IPO can proceed at the expected $1.5 trillion valuation. To be fair, the AI scare has been building for some time. Our columnist Prosenjit Datta pointed out on Monday, before the fears of Democratic Party restrictions on data centres emerged, that “tech stocks took a brutal beating on Wall Street last week as AI-bubble fears, soaring debt costs, and weak earnings triggered a panic-driven sell-off”. This week on Moneycontrol Pro, FT’s Martin Wolf set out the economic risks of the AI boom while Ruchir Sharma argued that AI mania can’t survive higher interest rates. As for the wild market swings in the US, every fund manager who held semiconductors on Tuesday and sold them on Thursday probably lives by Groucho Marx’s creed: “Those are my principles, and if you don’t like them, I have others”. Although this FT story argues that the chill US voters have put on the data centre boom may have a silver lining for investors. What with a war that may or may not be over and a technology that may or may not be a bubble, investors this week were reminded of TS Eliot’s famous line, “Humankind cannot bear very much reality”. No wonder the CBOE Vix shot up nearly 70 percent on Thursday. But it’s Diwali and the Indian markets thought it’s time to celebrate. FIIs are back and almost everybody is now saying India is an anti-AI hedge. Indeed, the Q2 corporate results read like the hedge in action. Among the heavyweights, SBI posted a steady quarter with a good outlook for the second half, RIL’s O2C business drove a record performance, and LIC is hoping better margins and business mix can earn it a re-rating. These are the domestic, balance-sheet-heavy names that the AI trade never had any use for, which is why a hedge buyer might. Moneycontrol Pro’s researchers have analysed many other such results — Their recommendations are listed in the ‘Stocks’ section at the end of this missive. In fact, things seem to be getting even better — The HSBC India Composite Purchasing Managers Index for October, a snapshot of seasonally adjusted month-on-month change in private sector activity, was 56.2 in October, up from an already decent 55.9 in September. Best of all, the aggregate rate of inflation retreated to the softest since February 2022. Combined with lower oil prices, lower bond yields, and a stronger rupee, there are good reasons for the market’s optimism. GST data show that domestic collections have improved steadily since May. The opposition to data centres in the US may lead to more of them coming to India, although that may be a mixed blessing. Festive demand lifted auto volumes in October, the global diesel crunch is actually good for India, and gold, the festival’s favourite metal, has been getting cheaper, thanks partly to a stronger rupee. The price drop will boost festive spirits. Sure, there’s plenty to be cautious about. El Nino hasn’t gone away, and there are signs of rural distress. There are concerns for Indian IT over AI disruption and spending. My colleague Vatsala Kamat interviewed Bernstein’s Venugopal Garre, who said we shouldn’t count on AI fatigue to lift the Indian markets. Nevertheless, our head of research Madhuchanda Dey points out that possible de-escalation in the Middle-East could be a game changer for India and that “the broad-based earnings revival seen in Q1 hasn’t got derailed in Q2 and the headline Nifty (@ 24,000) now trades at 19.2X FY27 estimated earnings for a mid-teen earnings growth and at a discount to its long-period average”. We have picked 17 stocks that combine growth potential with attractive valuations for your Diwali 2026 portfolio. As we step into Samvat 2083, perhaps the most important lesson is not to be swayed by sentiment. Another, as this piece points out, is that beer consumption in India is very low. I'm sure there's a lesson in that, especially in the festive season — and a case for urgent action. So light the lamps and enjoy the mithai. But keep your leverage low and your sense of humour high. As TS Eliot wrote at the end of ‘The Waste Land’: ‘Datta. Dayadhvam. Damyata. (For those of us unfamiliar with Sanskrit, that roughly means Give, Sympathise, Control.) Shantih, Shantih, Shantih.’ Shubh Deepavali, Manas Chakravarty In case you missed them, here are some of the other stories and insights we published this week, apart from our technical picks in the equity, commodity, and forex markets: Stocks Weekly Tactical Pick: Which capital market stock gains most from a Nifty revival?
