The innovative business is growing fast, but its valuation leaves little room for error. By Neil Patel – Nov 25, 2026 at 6:06AM EST +Fool.comonShare Summarize with AI Key Points Revenue surged 161% year over year in Q3, led by the booming AI segment and a highly profitable Starlink business. Capital spending is soaring, with Wall Street expecting free cash flow of negative $146 billion in 2027. Even with strong execution, Space Exploration Technologies' steep valuation could leave shareholders with disappointing returns over the next five years. 10 stocks we like better than Space Exploration Technologies › In its short history spanning less than six months as a publicly traded company, Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX +4.83%) shares have been as volatile as the rockets it launches. They quickly soared 25% from the initial public offering opening price to the record close on June 16. Since then, they've fallen 34% (as of Nov. 23). In addition to its space operations and connectivity segment, the business has developed a strong presence in the artificial intelligence (AI) industry. A lot of moving parts mean that SpaceX seems to be in the news all the time. Investors can find it challenging to focus less on the day-to-day updates and more on the big picture. When looking at SpaceX, it's important to apply a long-term filter. The company, which sports a significant market capitalization of $1.8 trillion, could be very different in the future. Where will this AI stock be in five years? Image source: The Motley Fool. AI drives the growth In the third quarter (ended Sept. 30), SpaceX's total revenue surged 161% year over year to $14.6 billion. Viewed from above, there are three distinct businesses. The superintelligence segment, the company's AI unit, is the biggest. Its revenue skyrocketed to $7.6 billion from $813 million a year earlier. Most of it comes from renting data center capacity to AI developers like Anthropic and Alphabet's Google Cloud. The Cursor coding tool and the Grok chatbot add to the total. Connectivity, which is primarily Starlink, brought in $5.2 billion, up 69%. With 13.6 million subscribers, it's the profit engine, generating $2.4 billion in Q3 operating income. The legacy rocket launch business, the space segment, posted $1.8 billion in revenue and essentially broke even. Fast-forward five years. The key financial drivers will be gigawatts of computing capacity, Starlink subscribers, and Starship's ability to lower launch costs. Management expects to add 10 gigawatts of compute in 2027 alone, up from 1.7 gigawatts at the end of September. It guided for Q1 2027 revenue of $33 billion to $37 billion. Morningstar projects $228 billion in sales in 2030. Elon Musk believes revenue could be $1 trillion that year. It won't be cheap to achieve this growth. Capital expenditures totaled $48.1 billion in the first nine months of 2026, with another $35 billion to $40 billion expected in Q4. Operating cash flow doesn't come close to covering this bill. The consensus estimate among sell-side analysts is that free cash flow will be negative $146 billion in 2027. The five-year question doesn't pertain to revenue. It's mainly about whether all this spending eventually produces cash. That's based on trailing-12-month revenue of $32 billion. That looks absurdly expensive. But backward-looking numbers can be misleading when a company changes this fast. Annualize the $35 billion midpoint of Q1 2027 guidance, and the P/S multiple falls to 12.9. Against Morningstar's 2030 forecast, it's just 7.9. That math is better the further out you look. For what it's worth, the competitive advantages are real. SpaceX lifted more than 80% of the world's mass to orbit in 2025, thanks to its reusable rockets. That cost edge lets it deploy Starlink satellites more cheaply than anyone else, building a network rivals would struggle to replicate. However, the economic moat comes from space-related endeavors. The growth is coming from AI, a far less defensible business. Anthropic, SpaceX's largest AI customer, can exit its agreements with 90 days' notice once their initial terms end in early 2027. Google's deal has a similar exit clause. This is the risk investors are probably overlooking the most. It makes any five-year outlook highly uncertain. You see this in the revenue forecasts. Add Starship execution risk and heavy cash burn, and the range of outcomes is enormous. Even with strong execution, SpaceX's steep valuation could leave shareholders with disappointing returns.
